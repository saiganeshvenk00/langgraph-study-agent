Geography is the study of the Earth's landscapes, environments, and the relationships between people and their surroundings. It is divided into physical geography, which examines natural systems, and human geography, which explores how people organise and interact with space and place.

The Earth's surface is constantly shaped by internal and external forces. Plate tectonics explains how the lithosphere is divided into large plates that move over the mantle, causing earthquakes, volcanic activity, and the formation of mountain ranges. Erosion, weathering, and deposition by water, wind, and ice further sculpt landforms such as valleys, deltas, and coastlines over geological time.

Climate and weather are central to physical geography. Climate is determined by latitude, altitude, proximity to oceans, and prevailing winds. The Earth has several climate zones including tropical, arid, temperate, and polar. Climate change, driven largely by increased greenhouse gas emissions from human activity, is altering weather patterns, raising sea levels, and threatening ecosystems worldwide.

Rivers are vital geographic features that shape landscapes and support human settlement. They flow from upland sources to the sea, passing through stages of erosion in upper courses and deposition in lower courses. Deltas and floodplains form at the mouths and lower reaches of major rivers. Freshwater from rivers is essential for agriculture, industry, and drinking water.

Human geography examines population distribution, migration, urbanisation, and economic development. Push and pull factors drive migration, with people leaving areas of hardship and moving toward opportunity. Cities grow as centres of economic activity, but rapid urbanisation creates challenges around housing, infrastructure, and inequality. Globalisation connects economies and cultures while also raising issues of sustainability and resource distribution.
